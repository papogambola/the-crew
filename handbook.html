What this is

Chapter 1 · The game in four minutes

1.1The game

You are the commander of a criminal crew. You are not the police, and there is nobody above you. Clients post work on a board — by country, by kind, with a fee on it — and you decide which of that work your crew takes, who goes on it, and what happens when it goes wrong in the middle.

A turn is a week. Taking a job spends one to three of them, casing a job spends one, laying low spends one. Every week the payroll comes out of your float whether anybody worked or not, the heat falls a little, and the world does something you did not ask it to.

There are 5,000 people on file. Five attributes each, a trade, a passport, a record, a price, and a secret. You will hire between four and six of them at a time, out of five thousand, and the difference between a good crew and a bad one is not their numbers — it is whether their numbers match the room you are sending them into.

1.2The goal

Build the name. Everything in the game is downstream of your ranking: what clients post to you, what they pay, who will sign with you, whether the crew stays loyal, and how hard the police look.

At ranking 420 the board has nothing left to offer you but the last score — three operations, in order, against the Committee. They are the room where the names are kept. Nobody robs them. Finish all three and the game is over, and you won it.

The last score does not appear until four things are true at once:

  • A name at the top of the board — ranking 420.
  • A career behind you — 55 jobs run.
  • A crew, not a pair — 5 people who can actually get in the door.
  • A war chest — $5M on hand. Nobody funds this one.

The Dashboard keeps that list ticked off as it arrives, under The operation. There is always a next thing to be working towards, and it is never a mystery what it is.

1.3How you lose

Nobody on the books, and not enough money to hire the cheapest file still available. That is the whole condition. You do not lose by being arrested — you lose by being unable to start again.

The ways people leave the books: they are hurt, they are held, they walk out over the money, they are poached by another crew, they take a cut of a score and vanish, you cut them loose, or they are killed. Every one of those leaves somebody outside your operation who knows things about it, which is its own problem — see loose ends.

1.4The three rules the game plays by

Everything has a cause and a consequence. No number on any screen appeared because it looked right. If a job says you are +9 on the night, there is a line in the factor list saying which week of casing bought it.

The odds are written on the option before you take it. Every twist option, every way of dealing with a loose end, every recruitment snag states its own percentage and its own cost. You are never asked to guess at a hidden number, only to decide whether you can afford the bad half.

Nothing is graded that you did not choose. The game does not invent a verdict. If it cannot know something, it says so rather than filling it in.

Where everything is

Chapter 2 · The screen, tab by tab

2.1The top bar

Pinned to the top of every screen, and it never scrolls away.

  • The name — your alias, and the ranking it currently carries.
  • Money — the float. Fees, cuts, upkeep, retainers, bribes and casing all come out of this one number.
  • Heat — how hard the world is looking, 0 to 120, drawn as a bar. See Heat and the law.
  • Week — the clock. Everything in the game is measured in weeks.

2.2The four tabs

Also pinned. Keys 1 2 3 4 jump straight to them.

TabWhat is on it
CrewWho is on the books: their cards, attributes, trade, loyalty, cut, upkeep, temperament, limits and what they have been through. Retainers and the safe house are reached from here too, and so is founding a second crew.
JobsThe board. Every posting open this week, its country, its fee, what it wants, and — once you open one — the job file: who can go, who cannot and why, the odds, the split, and the button that starts it.
RosterAll five thousand files, filtered. This is where hiring happens.
DashboardSix folded sections: the operation, the competition and the law, standing arrangements, the record, job recaps, and the case log. One opens at a time; opening one shuts the last.

2.3The office

Esc, or the ≡ button at the top right, on any screen. It pauses the game and opens the desk: sound, music, brightness, the controls book, your own file, the file cabinet, and the door out.

The i button beside it explains whatever is on screen, and can run the tutorial again — the whole thing, a short reminder, or any single card.

3

You

Chapter 3 · The commander

3.1Opening a dossier

A new game starts with the WHO ARE YOU? screen. You choose:

  • Alias — type one, or press ↻ RANDOM, which draws a name from the naming pool of the nationality you have picked. A Nigerian commander gets a Nigerian name.
  • Appearance — a face, drawn in ink. ↻ RANDOM here too.
  • Sex — male or female.
  • Nationality — which passport you carry, which decides which borders are a problem for you.
  • Trade — what you personally are. You count as that trade on every job, exactly as a hired member would.
  • Attributes — points to spend across the five.

You start with $150K and four soldiers' places to fill.

3.2Command

You have one number nobody else has: command. It starts at 5 and is added to the reckoning of every job you personally go on, on top of everything your attributes and trade are worth.

Recruits who are commander types are worth having and awkward to keep. On a job you are on, each one is −3 — they take orders badly. On a job you cannot get into, the best of them stands in and is worth their command −2, minimum 1. With nobody able to lead, the job is −10 before anybody has done anything.

3.3You are always the commander

There is no mechanism in the game for handing the operation to somebody else. A stand-in leader runs one night and hands it straight back. Every screen addresses you as the person who decides.

The people

Chapter 4 · Everything on a crew member's file

4.1The five attributes

Every file has five, roughly 20 to 100, and they are the base of everything. A job leans on some of them and ignores the rest — see the twelve kinds of job.

4.2The sixteen trades

A trade is what somebody is. It is not a bonus you can buy — it decides which jobs want them, what knowledge they arrive with, where they stand on the plan, and which options open up when a night goes sideways.

A member whose trade the client asked for is worth +14 on the reckoning, on top of their attributes. Several of the trades also unlock answers nothing else can give you: a Forger gets people across a border they would be turned back from, a Cleaner is the only person who can make a problem an accident, an Enforcer is the only one who can have a conversation, and a Fixer can arrange for somebody to be somewhere else for a long time.

4.7Limits

What somebody will not do. A limit is absolute — no bonus, no loyalty and no amount of money moves it, and a member whose limit the job hits simply does not go. A crew of six where four are claustrophobic is a crew of two underground.

Limits are the single most common reason a job cannot be run at full strength, and the Who goes list on every job file names the limit by name against the person it stops.

4.8Passport, nationality and the border

Everybody carries a passport, and passports are not equal. Countries are rated by how hard they are to enter: open, needs a visa, or strict.

  • A local national always gets in, and is worth +8 to the job for knowing the ground.
  • A strict border turns back anyone whose passport is better than strong.
  • A border that needs a visa turns back anyone whose passport is weak.
  • Time inside counts against you at a border: three years for a strict country, five for a visa country.
  • A Forger on the crew — one who is not themselves barred from that country — clears all of it. That is what a Forger is for.

4.9Languages

Somebody on the crew who speaks the local language is worth +6. Nobody who does is −8 — or −14 if the job is one of the talking kinds, where the whole thing is a conversation and there is no version of it that works in mime.

4.10What people cost you

MoneyWhenWhat it is
The feeOnce, when they signWhat it takes to get them on the books. Scales with experience, attributes, knowledge, schooling and whether their trade is a scarce one.
The cutEvery job they go onA percentage of the whole fee, off the top, before you see any of it. Roughly 3% at Rookie, rising about 1.2 points per experience rank, with a little jitter. A Mercenary takes 2 points more; somebody who demands 15% takes at least that.

Miss the payroll and everybody on the books loses 9 loyalty, every retainer stops answering, and the float is set to zero. It is the worst single thing that can happen to you without a job being involved.

4.11Loyalty

0 to 100, and it starts somewhere between 42 and 68. It decides whether somebody stays when another crew buys them a drink, and how likely they are to talk once they are off the books.

What moves itHow much
A messy job−3 to everyone who went
A botched job−7
A blown job — a disaster−13
A missed payroll−9 to everybody on the books
A bonus+14, and three weeks paid up
Your rankingNobody: −1 a week. Known: nothing. Respected and up: +1. Untouchable and up: +2.
Being cut loose−6, and then they are a loose end

Paid up is worth having for reasons beyond the loyalty: for 3 weeks after a bonus they are worth +2 on every reckoning, deaf to other crews, and not the one trouble picks on.

Restless is the opposite. A cut worth less than a week of what they cost you is a job they did for you rather than for themselves. Three of those in a row and they grumble — −2 each on every job — and five and they may walk.

4.12Greed, moles and vetting

Roughly one file in fourteen belongs to an informant. They look like everybody else. A mole on the books leaks +3 heat a week, unexplained, and is very much more likely to talk once they are off the books.

Vetting costs $6K and can be done from the roster before you ever fly out to meet somebody. It usually finds them. It is less reliable when nobody on your crew is sharp — with no one above 55 Brains in the field there is about a one-in-three chance a mole comes back clean.

Greed is a hidden number between 15 and 90. It does not change what they are worth on a job. It changes what they do when the job is over: a greedy member is likelier to take a cut of the score and vanish, and likelier to talk afterwards.

5

Hiring

Chapter 5 · The roster, the trip, the signature

5.1The roster

Five thousand files, filterable by trade, experience, nationality, price and what they will not do. They are generated once from the game's seed and they do not change — the person you looked at in week 3 and could not afford is still there in week 40, at the same price, unless somebody else got to them.

5.2Who will sign

Your ranking decides the ceiling. A Nobody cannot get a Legend to sit down with them at any price.

  • Nobody and Small time: nobody above Professional.
  • Known: up to Veteran.
  • Respected and above: anybody, including Legends.

Somebody who turns you down will not meet you again for a set number of weeks, and the roster says which week.

5.3The recruitment trip

You do not click hire and get a person. You fly out to where they are and sit down with them, and it runs as a live report exactly like a job does: a clock in the corner, a map of the city with the meeting on it, and the thing unfolding line by line.

  1. You land, and the city is described as it actually is — its money, its weather, its politics.
  2. You walk it for a day. What you notice depends on who you brought.
  3. You sit down. Speaking the language, having a Face along, being a name people have heard of — each of them changes how the table goes.
  4. Something happens. Not always, but often.
  5. They sign, or they do not.

Nothing is paid until the signature. The fee leaves your float at the end of the meeting and not before, so a trip that ends in nothing ends in nothing — plus whatever the snag itself cost you.

5.4The snag

Five kinds, and which one you get depends on who you are meeting:

SnagWhen it happens
Somebody else is at the tableAnyone. A rival crew got there first.
They want to test youOnly the good ones — Veteran and Legend.
It is a set-upGreedy, or an informant, or six years inside or more.
They are not what the file saysLow experience, or low loyalty. Usually drunk.
A check at the hotelAny country that needs a visa, is strict, or is under authoritarian rule.

Every snag offers several ways out, and which ones are open depends on what you brought with you — a Forger's papers, a Face making a call, an Enforcer, the local language, or simply a record long enough that they have heard of you. There is always at least one option available, and walking away is always one of them.

5.5Cutting somebody loose

Dropping a member costs them 6 loyalty and puts them straight back on the roster at their original price. It also makes them a loose end, which is a separate conversation that the game will make you have.

6

The crew

Chapter 6 · Places, who goes, and a second crew

6.1Places

You start with four soldiers' places and yourself — five. A Respected name (ranking 65) opens two more, for six soldiers and you. That is the ceiling: 6 soldiers, plus the commander.

6.2Who goes

Everybody on the books goes on every job, unless something stops them. There is no picking a team. What there is instead is a list of reasons somebody cannot go:

  • A limit the job hits — the tag is named against them.
  • A border their passport or their record will not get them through.
  • They are injured, and the file says which week they are back.
  • They are held by the police.
  • You benched them.

The job file states, at the top, how many the client needs and how many can actually go. Under the needed number the whole crew is −22 per missing body, which is enormous — it is usually the single largest number in the factor list, and it is the reason a job you are short for is not worth running at any fee.

6.3Benching

Benching somebody keeps their percentage of the fee. That is all it does.

It does not save their wages. The payroll is charged every week for everybody on the books, benched or not, for as many weeks as the job holds the crew. The job file shows both numbers side by side — what the cuts cost and what the weeks cost — for exactly this reason.

Benching also lowers the crew size bonus, and it removes whatever that person was bringing: their trade, their language, their knowledge, their passport. The file recalculates the odds live as you bench people, so you can see the trade being made rather than guessing at it.

6.4Injured and held

A bad night puts people in hospital or in a cell. A doctor on retainer halves the weeks of an injury; a lawyer on retainer halves the weeks of a sentence. Somebody who comes out of a cell is back on the roster rather than on your crew, a year older, with that year on their record — and a record is what borders read.

6.5A second crew

From two years in, with a big enough name and a full crew, you can found a second crew that works without you.

NeededValue
Week104 for the second, 156 for the third, 208 for the fourth — and no more
Rankingat least 80
Your crewfull — all 6 soldiers plus you
Among thema commander type, and 5 ready to go

A crew is a commander and four soldiers, named after its commander. It takes a posting of its own each week from the same board. Its take is yours, its ranking is yours, its heat is yours — and so is its payroll.

Chapter 7 · Where the work comes from

7.1The postings

Seven postings are kept open at all times — nine with a fixer on retainer, and one of those nine is written for the crew you actually have. They expire. A posting you were saving for next week may not be there next week.

Clients post to a name. A Nobody sees tier 1 and 2 only; a Known crew sees tier 3; Respected and above see everything. A client you have botched a job for stops posting to you at all.

7.2Tiers

Four of them, and the tier decides the fee, how many weeks it holds the crew, how many people the client needs, and how long you are allowed to sit outside it first.

7.5Countries

Forty-nine of them, and each one is a real place with its own languages, currency, economy, politics, terrain, cities and border. Which kinds of job appear there follows from what the country is.

Local heat is separate from your own. Working a country adds +14 to it and it falls 1 a week. Every point of it makes jobs in that country +0.34 harder. Work the same country five weeks running and you have made it your hardest country.

7.6Casing a job

A room you have watched is not the room you were told about. Each week of casing is worth +9 on the night.

  • It costs 6% of the fee, minimum $4K.
  • It spends a week — the payroll is charged, the world moves.
  • It adds +2 heat and +3 local heat.
  • You may case a tier-1 posting once and a tier-4 posting four times.
  • The posting can be gone when you look up. The rival works the same board, and clients stop waiting.

7.7How hard a job is

Every posting carries a difficulty, and the crew has to beat it. It is built like this:

PartValue
Base25
Per tier+16 × tier

Your ranking+19 per 100 ranking, slightly sub-linear — at 420 the world is about +62 harder
Local heat+0.34 a point

The ranking term is the important one, and it is the game's spine: the rooms you are offered are defended against the name you have built. A crew that does not keep improving does not stand still — it slides.

The reckoning

Chapter 8 · Exactly how a job is decided

8.1What one person is worth

For the job in front of them — not in general:

1. Take their five attributes, weighted by what this kind of job leans on, and divide by the total weight.
2. Multiply by 1 + (experience − 1) × 0.11.
3. Add 14 if the client asked for their trade.
4. Add education × 1.2 if this job's Brains weight is 3 or more.

The crew's power is the average of that across everybody who actually goes. An average, not a sum — which is why one brilliant specialist does not carry four passengers, and why adding a body who is wrong for the job makes the crew worse rather than bigger.

8.3The night

Add the factors to the power. Subtract the job's difficulty. That is the margin — what the job file shows as the odds.

On the night, a roll of ±26 is added to the margin, and where it lands decides the verdict. The width of that roll is why the file quotes five percentages rather than a yes or a no: a margin of +20 is a good job that can still be messy.

VerdictThe nightPaysRankingHeatFallout

A clean night pays 8% over the fee — the client is pleased and rounds up. A messy one pays 60%. A botched or blown one pays nothing at all, and the wages for those weeks still come out of your float.

8.4What a job leaves behind

Every verdict moves more than the money:

  • Ranking up or down, damped by how far beneath your name the job was. A tier-1 posting run by a Notorious crew barely registers.
  • Heat, the job's own figure multiplied by the verdict's own multiplier and again by your ranking's.
  • Local heat in that country, +14.
  • Experience to everyone who went, doubled for a Quick study, and half again for everyone on a job with a Mentor on it.
  • Attributes, occasionally, on whatever the job leaned on.
  • People hurt or held, on a botched or blown night.
  • Loyalty, down, on anything worse than a success.
  • Bad blood or a bond, between every pair who were there.
  • A client with a grudge, if you blew their job — and they may come back for it.
9

The night itself

Chapter 9 · The live report

9.1Reading the report

A job does not resolve into a number. It plays out: a clock in the top left, a line at a time, with the crew named and what each of them is doing named with them. You can run it at five speeds, or press through it as fast as you can read.

9.2The map

Beside the report is the world, drawn in ink — the country the job is in, with the sites of the job on it and your people standing at them, as faces, with a small symbol for what they are doing.

The plan has five positions, and which one somebody stands at follows from their trade:

On the planWho stands there
The streetwhoever the line is about
The postHacker, Fixer, Lookout, Launderer, Overwatch
The way inGrifter, Forger, Infiltrator, Pickpocket, Face
The roomSafecracker, Enforcer, Demolitions
The carWheelman, Cleaner, Smuggler

A recruitment trip gets the same treatment, with four positions instead: landing, the city, the table, and the deal.

9.3The twist

Most jobs go wrong in the middle. The report stops, states the problem, and offers up to six ways out — each one with the odds on it and a requirement written into it. One of them is always something your crew can actually do.

The options are gated on what you brought: a trade, an attribute above a threshold, the language, or a long enough record. Choosing the option that fits your crew is worth a great deal; choosing the impressive one you cannot back up is how a good job becomes a blown one.

A twist you walk away from mid-decision is not lost. Close the game in the middle of it and it resumes exactly there — the report so far, then the options.

9.4WHAT WENT WRONG

When a job ends botched or blown, the report adds a section that reads back the actual reckoning and says, in order of how much it cost, what beat you — and for each of those, what would have fixed it. Not advice in general: the trade that was wanted and missing, the language nobody spoke, the domain nobody knew, the bodies you were short, the weeks you did not spend watching it, the heat you carried in.

10

Money

Chapter 10 · Where it goes

10.1The split

The fee is not yours. Everybody who goes takes their percentage off the top and you keep the rest. The job file shows the whole thing before you commit:

  • Every person who is going, with their percentage and what that is in money.
  • What the crew takes altogether, and what is left for you.
  • The wages — the weekly payroll and every retainer, multiplied by the weeks the job holds the crew.
  • What you should expect to keep: every verdict's takings weighted by its own odds, with the crew's percentage off each, minus the wages. A botched job pays nothing but still costs its share of the odds — which is the whole reason benching for a bigger share can leave you with less.

10.2Standing arrangements

11

Heat and the law

Chapter 11 · Being looked for

11.1Heat

A number from 0 to 120 measuring how hard the world is looking at you. Jobs add it. It falls 5 a week by itself, 7, 9 or 11 with a safe house.

Heat costs you −0.24 on every job, per point — at heat 80 every job is −19 before anybody has done anything. It is not a punishment meter; it is a difficulty dial that you turn yourself.

Laying low spends a week for −15 heat and −6 off the detective's file, and costs one point of ranking. A name unused is a name forgotten.

11.2The detective

Once your heat has been up, or your name is big enough, somebody opens a file on your alias. They have a name, and the Dashboard tells you how thick the file is.

  • It thickens by roughly heat ÷ 22 a week, and faster above heat 70.
  • Below heat 25 it thins by 0.9 a week. A quiet month is a cold case.
  • At 50 they can brief a squad. At 75 they can do it faster.
  • Paying a desk, laying low, and a raid itself all thin it.

11.3The raid

A raid comes when heat reaches 100 — or 90 once the file is at 50, or 80 once it is at 75. It takes one or two of your crew into a cell, takes 30% of your float — 15% with the best safe house — and drops your heat to 58.

A thick file does not raid you. It brings the raid forward. Those are different problems and they have different answers: heat is bought down with a bribe or a quiet week, a file is bought down with a bribe or time.

12

The competition

Chapter 12 · Somebody else is working the board

12.1The other outfit

Once you reach ranking 20, somebody else appears on the same board with their own boss, their own name and their own standing. They are not an event — they are a second player.

  • Most weeks they take a posting off the board, and it is the best one left.
  • Every posting they take raises their standing.
  • When their standing is ahead of yours they take more often, and they start buying your crew drinks.

12.2Being poached

While another crew is buying drinks, your least loyal member who is not paid up has a real chance of walking every week. Loyalty below 60 is the danger zone. A bonus makes somebody deaf to it for 3 weeks — which is the cheapest counter in the game.

12.3What you can do about them

  • Buy a name inside — costs at least $40K, or their standing × $3K, and takes 8 to 14 points off them. No heat.
  • Send the enforcer — free, needs an Enforcer on the books. 65% it takes 12 to 20 points off them for +6 heat. 35% it goes loud in a street with windows: +16 heat, and they are not frightened.
13

Between jobs

Chapter 13 · The week that passes on its own

13.1What a week does

  1. The payroll is charged — every member's upkeep and every retainer.
  2. Moles leak +3 heat each.
  3. Loyalty drifts by whatever your ranking is worth.
  4. Another crew's drinks may take somebody.
  5. The injured come back and the held walk out, if their week has come.
  6. Heat falls. Local heat falls by 1 everywhere.
  7. Weeks without a job accumulate; 3 of them costs a point of ranking.
  8. The rival takes a posting. The detective writes.
  9. Loose ends, grudges and revenge tick over.
  10. A raid, if the heat has reached the number.
  11. Every 3 to 7 weeks, something that is not a job.

13.2Things that are not jobs

Eight kinds, each with several ways out, each with its odds written on it.

When somebody threatens to walk, their whole file opens underneath the question — attributes, trade, experience, loyalty, what they have been through — so the decision is made about a person rather than a name. One of the options is to let them go and settle it in forty-eight hours.

13.3Loose ends

Anybody who leaves the books alive knows things. The game will not let you ignore it — the question is put to you, once, per person, with the odds.

How they leftChance they talk, before adjustments
Walked out over the money26%
Took a cut of the score and vanished46%
Left for another crew38%
Asked to be let out18%
Was cut loose20%

That figure is then raised by what they know, a long record, greed, and being an informant — a mole adds 20 points — and lowered by their loyalty. A Ghost is less likely to talk; a Hothead more.

The ways of dealing with it run from doing nothing to a contract, and every one of them names its own price, its own odds and what it costs you with the crew. The quiet answers need a specific trade on the books: a Cleaner, an Enforcer, or a Fixer. Without them, the list is much shorter and much worse.

13.4Grudges and revenge

Blow a client's job and there is a real chance they take it personally — 62% on a disaster, 34% on a botch, 5% on a messy night.

  • They stop posting to you, for 16 to 34 weeks.
  • They may take one or two of their friends with them — other clients who stop posting too.
  • And 42% of the time they do not merely stop. Something arrives, 2 to 6 weeks later.

Automatic, in this browser, after everything. Settings are kept separately and survive a new game.

A game lives in one browser only. Clear its data, play on another machine, or lose the laptop, and the crew goes with it. 18

Twenty things worth knowing

Chapter 18 · Everything above, put to use
  1. Read the category before the fee. The twelve kinds of job lean on different attributes. A crew of muscle is ordinary at anything the client wants talked through.
  2. Being short is worse than being weak. −22 a body dwarfs everything else in the factor list. A job you cannot field is a job to skip, whatever it pays.
  3. Hire a Forger early. Not for the +14 — for the borders. One Forger turns a crew of six passports into a crew that can work anywhere.
  4. Limits are permanent. Check them before you sign, not the first time a posting is tagged underground and four of your people stay in the car.
  5. Vet the cheap ones. $6K against a mole who leaks heat every week and is very likely to talk on the way out is not a close call.
  6. Somebody sharp makes vetting work. With nobody above 55 Brains in the field, a third of moles come back clean.
  7. Bonuses are the cheapest defence you have. 3 weeks of +2 on every job, deafness to other crews, and immunity from being the one trouble picks on.
  8. Watch the small cuts. Three jobs in a row where somebody's percentage is worth less than a week of their wages and they start grumbling; five and they walk.
  9. Case the big ones. +9 a week for 6% of the fee is the best rate in the game — but the posting can be gone when you look up.
  10. Do not work the same country twice running. +14 local heat a job, falling 1 a week, and every point of it is difficulty.
  11. Heat is a difficulty dial, not a punishment. At 80 every job is −19. Buy it down before a big one, not after a raid.
  12. A thick file does not raid you — it moves the raid closer. Heat and the file are two numbers with two different answers.
  13. Benching saves the cut and not the wages. The whole payroll is charged for every week the job runs, benched or not.
  14. The expected-keep figure is the honest one. It weights every verdict by its own odds and subtracts the wages. A bigger share of a job you will probably botch is less money.
  15. Take the twist option your crew can actually back up. Every option states what it needs. The impressive one you cannot back is how a good job becomes a blown one.
  16. Read WHAT WENT WRONG. It is not flavour — it names the factor that cost you the most and what would have fixed it.
  17. Keep pairs together. A pair gains a point for every job that comes off with both of them on it. A long-running crew is worth real numbers.
  18. Bad blood is the same mechanism in reverse. Hover it and the game tells you which nights caused it.
  19. Your ranking makes the world harder. +19 difficulty per 100. A crew that stops improving is sliding.
  20. Start building for the last score before it opens. It wants Hacker, Infiltrator, Forger, Safecracker, Demolitions, Overwatch, Smuggler, Wheelman and Cleaner across three operations — and $5M of your own money.
